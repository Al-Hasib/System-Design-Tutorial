# Why This Topic Matters: Design a URL Shortener

> **In one sentence:** This is the "hello world" of system design interviews — small enough that you can finish it in 40 minutes, and deep enough that it forces you to confront ID generation, extreme read/write skew, and the difference between a cache being nice and a cache being load-bearing.

## Why This Case Study Exists

A URL shortener sounds trivial: store a mapping, redirect on lookup. That apparent simplicity is exactly why it is the standard opening problem. There is no domain complexity to hide behind, so the conversation goes straight to the fundamentals — and an interviewer can see very quickly whether a candidate estimates before designing, or starts drawing boxes.

It is also the problem where the gap between the naive answer and the good answer is largest relative to the effort. "Hash the URL and store it" is a five-second answer that falls apart on the first follow-up.

## The Design Problems It Forces You to Solve

### 1. Generating short, unique IDs without coordination
**The problem:** You need a 6-8 character code that is unique across billions of URLs, generated by many servers simultaneously, with no collisions and no central bottleneck.

**Why it is hard:** An auto-incrementing database ID gives uniqueness but requires a single sequence — a coordination point and a scaling limit — and it makes codes guessable and enumerable. Hashing the long URL produces collisions that must be detected and resolved, which means a read before every write. A random code requires a uniqueness check that gets slower as the keyspace fills.

**What you learn:** The counter-plus-base62 approach and why base62 gives you 62^7 ≈ 3.5 trillion codes in seven characters. Pre-allocating ID ranges per server (a ticket server or a Snowflake-style scheme) to remove the per-write coordination. And the security consequence — sequential codes are enumerable, so anyone can walk your entire database — which is a genuinely good example of a non-functional requirement changing a technical choice.

### 2. A read/write ratio of 100:1 or worse
**The problem:** Writes are rare (creating a link) and reads are enormous (every click, forever, often in bursts when a link goes viral).

**Why it is hard:** Optimizing both is a different architecture than optimizing one.

**What you learn:** This is the cleanest illustration of asymmetric optimization in the whole course. Reads get aggressive caching (the working set of popular links is tiny and the data is immutable, so cache invalidation — usually the hard part — barely exists here), read replicas, and a CDN or edge layer for the redirect itself. Writes get a simple, correct path. Recognizing and naming the skew before designing is the single highest-scoring move in this problem.

### 3. Capacity estimation that actually informs the design
**The problem:** How much storage for five years? How many requests per second at peak? Does this fit on one database?

**Why it is hard:** Candidates either skip it or compute numbers they then ignore.

**What you learn:** The habit of estimating and then *using* the result. A few hundred bytes per record times a few billion records is a few hundred gigabytes — which comfortably fits on one machine, meaning the honest answer is that you may not need sharding at all. Being willing to conclude "this does not need to be complicated" is a strong signal, and the estimate is what earns you the right to say it.

### 4. The details that separate a complete answer
**The problem:** Custom aliases, link expiry, analytics on clicks, and the choice of HTTP redirect status.

**What you learn:** Custom aliases need a uniqueness check and a reserved-words list. Expiry needs a TTL and a cleanup strategy (lazy deletion or a background job). Analytics must not be on the synchronous redirect path — publish a click event to a queue and process it asynchronously, or you have coupled your fastest operation to your slowest. And 301 versus 302 is a real trade-off: a permanent redirect is cached by browsers and is therefore faster and cheaper, but you will never see those clicks again, so if analytics matter you use 302.

## What It Costs to Get Wrong

- **Skipping estimation** leads to designing a sharded, multi-region system for something that fits on one node — and interviewers read that as an inability to right-size.
- **Hashing the URL** without addressing collisions is the most common incomplete answer.
- **Putting analytics in the redirect path** turns a sub-10 ms operation into one that depends on your analytics pipeline's availability.
- **Ignoring enumeration** means a design that leaks every link anyone has ever created.
- **Over-engineering** — proposing Kafka, microservices, and a custom consensus layer for a key-value lookup — is judged more harshly than under-engineering, because it suggests reflex rather than reasoning.

## Why Interviewers Choose This One

It is the most common warm-up in the industry, and it is a calibration tool. Within a few minutes the interviewer knows whether you clarify requirements first, whether you estimate, whether you can identify the dominant access pattern, and whether you can resist adding complexity. Every one of those behaviors matters more in the harder problems that follow, which is why this one comes first.
